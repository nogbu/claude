## Research; history; 2026-10-10; read before turning findings into requirements

Research (quick pass, 2026-10-09; Reddit was blocked from this thread, so these are retailer FAQs, reviews and blog comments)  
- Drying: sphagnum poles dry out fast, "sometimes within 24 hours" (treleaf.shop); round open poles dry faster than D-shaped ones with a solid back (gardeningbeyond.com). Keeping several poles damp is the chore people complain about most.  
- Mold, musty smell and fungus gnats come from poles kept soggy with poor airflow (sybotanica.com).  
- Coir needs less watering but sheds and breaks down in 12–18 months (gardeningbeyond.com); some find coir doesn't stay moist enough for roots (ohiotropics.com comments).  
- Stability: short stakes (4–5 in) let tall poles lean; heavy pots help; a 3D printed brace that expands in the pot is sold (soil.ninja); thin or bendable cores bow under mature plants.  
- Extending: people want poles that stack as the plant grows; poles that don't stack, or mesh and backing of mismatched lengths, draw complaints. Printed poles screw or twist-lock together (soil.ninja, MakerWorld 1953546).  
- Adding to a planted pot is awkward; most advise doing it at repotting. A soil spike for adding without repotting is sold (soil.ninja).  
- Flat surfaces: flat D-shape mesh gives aerial roots broad contact (gardeningbeyond.com); flat poles suit shingling plants such as Monstera dubia and climbing Anthuriums (sybotanica.com). Cedar planks need no wetting and roots attach well at about 50% humidity or more, but they rot and can't be extended (treleaf.shop).  
- Liked: a translucent back to see roots and moisture, a solid back that slows drying, a funnel cap for watering from the top, refillable moss.  
- Other wishes: thinner, more discreet poles; sphagnum is hard to find locally; one printed pole's buyers report breakage after a year and a brace that fits only pots up to 28 cm.

Research (forum pass, 2026-10-10; Reddit blocks cloud servers, so Garden.org forums, Greg app community threads and MakerWorld and Printables pages, read through search summaries only, since this environment blocks fetching those sites). New beyond the quick pass:  
- Root attachment: several growers say indoor poles "rarely work" because aerial roots grip only when the moss stays constantly damp and the nodes are held against it (garden.org). Others keep poles dry and the plant still climbs, using the pole only as support (greg.app). Keeping poles wet with nutrient solution is said to give bigger leaves but is "a lot of upkeep" (greg.app). The sources disagree on whether roots grip coir.  
- Watering mess: wetting a pole soaks the table and floor; people rig a bottle or drip feeder with holes on top, or a wick from a container, and bottles still splash (greg.app; a drip bottle on a DIY pole, garden.org).  
- Mold on the moss surface is common; fixes are diluted hydrogen peroxide spray and a fan. One user says a solid, non-airy pole stays wet too long, which conflicts with the quick pass's liked solid back (greg.app).  
- Moss degrades, so the plant eventually needs fresh moss (garden.org).  
- Size: heavy climbers need a 3–5 in wide support, not a thin pole (garden.org).  
- Leaning: blamed on shallow pots; fixes are setting the pole deeper, rocks around its base and a bigger pot. Put the pole behind the plant where aerial roots come out and tie the stem loosely to it; soft ties or plant Velcro are used (greg.app).  
- Replacing or moving: attached roots are very tough, so removing a pole damages the plant and sometimes means chopping it into cuttings; a Monstera dubia that outgrew its plank was moved to a longer plank (garden.org, greg.app). Set a plank before the soil and plant (whyfarmit.com).  
- Printed poles (makers' pages and comments): print failures, supports and higher z-hop needed, brims for bed adhesion, bad fit when assembled the wrong way, delicate bottom spikes, tight threads, and scaling breaks the design (MakerWorld 38377, 440452, 27270; Printables 316281).
